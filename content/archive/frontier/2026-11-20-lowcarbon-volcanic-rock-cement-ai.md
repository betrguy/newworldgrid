### **The High Ground**

The Philippines is exploring a potential business partnership with SpaceX to develop rocket launch sites, as announced by President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. on November 20, 2026. This initiative aims to position the country as a key player in satellite and small payload launches. Additionally, ProLogium presented its Battery Pack 2.0 at the NASA Aerospace Battery Workshop for space missions and lunar infrastructure, highlighting advancements in battery technology for space applications.

### **The EV Race**

Niagara County dealership is planning to build an EV servicing and charging facility, as reported by the Buffalo News. This development indicates growing consumer demand for convenient on-site maintenance and charging options. For buyers and grid operators, this means easier access to EV services, potentially reducing the hassle of finding charging stations.

*What follows is purely a thought experiment.*

Imagine it’s 2036, and the Philippine Space Agency (PhilSA) is finally launching its first satellite, with SpaceX’s assistance. The launch marks a significant milestone for the Philippines, but here’s the twist: the satellite isn’t just for communications or Earth observation. It’s equipped with a miniaturized nuclear reactor, a technology previously seen only in the secretive space programs of the U.S. and Russia. This reactor powers a suite of advanced sensors designed to monitor global climate patterns, a mission PhilSA claims will be shared with NASA.

However, the real reason for the reactor’s inclusion is far more strategic. The nuclear power generates surplus energy that can be beamed back to Earth, potentially disrupting the global energy market. ProLogium, the company behind the battery technology showcased at the NASA workshop, is now part of a consortium bidding for contracts to build a space-based power relay station. This could shift the balance of power in the space economy, creating a new class of “energy satellites” that could challenge traditional energy suppliers. The implications are vast: countries dependent on fossil fuels might see their energy costs plummet, while existing energy companies might face unprecedented competition. This unexpected shift could spark a global race to claim orbital real estate and control these new energy sources.
