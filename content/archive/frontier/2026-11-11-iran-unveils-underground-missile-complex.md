### **The High Ground**

Iran’s new underground missile city, revealed as a warning to the U.S., highlights the evolving threat landscape. This development adds complexity to space security, particularly given Russia’s expansion of its CSTO satellite network for energy and compute. As a result, enhanced cybersecurity measures for space assets are likely to become more robust, potentially impacting the orbital economy.

### **The EV Race**

BYD's new Abu Dhabi site integrates solar energy, automated EV charging, and high-speed megawatt charging. This combination underscores the company's commitment to sustainable and efficient EV charging solutions. For buyers and grid operators, this means more reliable and eco-friendly charging options, which could help reduce strain on local power grids and accelerate the adoption of electric vehicles.

In 2036, the orbital economy has grown to include a constellation of private space stations and satellite networks owned by various companies, including OneWeb, SpaceX, and Amazon. However, a strange twist emerges: a new company, named Celestia Orbital Services (COS), begins to lease unused satellite slots from these established players. COS’s mission is not to launch satellites but to create an orbital “hotspot” network. Each slot becomes a node in a global quantum communication network, linking key cities and nations directly via quantum entanglement. This network, while initially costly at $10 million per slot, quickly becomes a strategic necessity for governments and corporations seeking ultra-secure communications. The unexpected demand for these slots causes a ripple effect: OneWeb and SpaceX, to maintain their market share, must either develop their own quantum communication capabilities or lease their unused slots to COS. The result is a new layer of competition and cooperation in the orbital economy, reshaping the balance of power in global communications.
