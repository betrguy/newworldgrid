### **The High Ground**

China’s first crewed lunar mission marks a significant milestone in national space programs. Three astronauts are set to land, underscoring China’s growing capabilities in human missions and lunar expeditions. Additionally, reports suggest that Houthis might be using Russian satellites to their advantage in conflicts, highlighting the complex use of orbital infrastructure.

### **The EV Race**

Tesla and Rivian are set to compete in Halloween mode, according to a report in USA Today. Tesla's latest model can travel 300 miles in this special mode, while Rivian's vehicle can manage 250 miles. This means Tesla has a slight edge in performance. Manufacturers will need to continue innovating to stay competitive, and grid operators should prepare for higher demand, particularly as more EV owners charge during peak hours.

*What follows is purely a thought experiment.*

Imagine it's 2036, and a mysterious company called Orbital Dynamics has quietly acquired a constellation of old Russian satellites, now repurposed for a unique mission. These satellites aren't just for communication; they've been retrofitted with advanced sensor arrays and maneuverable thrusters, effectively turning them into a floating swarm of space-based drones. The real kicker? These drones are equipped with laser technology capable of disabling smaller, less protected satellites, including those used by commercial providers and even military assets.

The strategic implications are staggering. With this capability, Orbital Dynamics could disrupt the space economy by targeting competitor satellites, potentially leading to a domino effect where smaller companies are forced to either develop their own defensive systems or rely on more robust providers. This scenario could also strain international relations, especially with nations like Russia and Saudi Arabia, which might see this as a violation of space law and a direct threat to their own satellite programs.

The economic impact could be equally profound. Satellite insurance premiums could skyrocket, and companies might be forced to rethink their orbital strategies, possibly leading to a consolidation of the space industry. In a world where space is becoming increasingly congested, such a development could accelerate the race for orbital supremacy.
