### **The High Ground**

Russia is expanding its orbital infrastructure through the CSTO, as evidenced by a chronicleonline.com report from November 10, 2026. This development includes the deployment of more satellites for space-based energy and compute, enhancing Russia's capabilities in the orbital industry. Additionally, Saudi Arabia is responding to missile attacks by the Houthi group with emergency measures to protect its space assets, as reported by Middle East Eye on the same day. These actions underscore the increasing importance of robust orbital security and infrastructure in the space economy.

### **The EV Race**

Ford’s new 30% more efficient EV platform, reported on November 8, 2026, is gaining traction. This advancement could shift buyer preferences, offering better range and performance. Barclays adjusted its investment outlook, adding Nio, XPeng, and Rivian while cutting Tesla, Lucid, and GM shares, suggesting that investors are showing interest in companies with superior efficiency and performance in electric vehicles.

*What follows is purely a thought experiment.*

Imagine it’s 2040, and the global space economy has become a bustling metropolis. Companies like SpaceX and Blue Origin are not just launching rockets but operating vast orbital infrastructure. One day, a small, unassuming startup called Orbital Dynamics, founded by a former NASA engineer, emerges with a revolutionary idea. They propose using repurposed missile defense interceptors to create a new type of space debris cleanup system.

This idea catches on, and suddenly, the U.S. Department of Defense, seeing the potential strategic benefits, invests heavily. But here’s the twist: the interceptors, originally designed to track and destroy hostile missiles, now have the ability to precisely map the orbital environment. This newfound capability allows the DoD to monitor not just space debris but also the movements of every satellite in low Earth orbit, including those of other nations.

The consequences are profound. Nations are forced to declare their satellite locations publicly, leading to a new form of transparency in space. This shift not only makes the orbital economy more stable but also shifts the balance of power in international relations. Space, once a realm of secretive and competitive nation-states, becomes a shared resource, governed by new, transparent protocols. The simple act of intercepting missiles has transformed the space race into a cooperative effort, with economic and strategic repercussions that ripple through global politics and technology.
